**The Scramble for the City: Fragmented Authority and the Struggle for Public Space in Mexico City** (book manuscript in progress)

This book traces how Mexico City's democratic transition, beginning in the late 1990s, transformed rather than resolved authoritarian forms of distributing public resources. Scholars have argued that democratization and decentralization create more accountable governance. This project shows that they also produce coordination failures, and that old ways of doing politics prove remarkably sticky as formal institutions change.

The book focuses on the middle layer of urban governance: street vending associations and bureaucrats who operate between residents and politicians, navigating overlapping jurisdictions and shifting political loyalties. Under the PRI, access to a selling spot ran through organizations affiliated with the party's popular sector, where affiliation was the price of the spot and loyalty, rally attendance, and votes were the return. Democratization broke this monopoly without replacing the channel. Organizations multiplied and atomized, and the number of actors who can grant access to a sidewalk rose along with the number who can extract from it.

I argue that democratization generated coordination failures along two axes. Horizontally, borough offices, city secretariats, inspectors, police, and organizations hold partial claims over the same block. Vertically, boroughs and the city compete over territory and over what the trade yields. The two are entangled, since horizontal fragmentation lets organizations and bureaucrats play offices against one another while vertical misalignment removes incentives to resolve the resulting disputes. A second argument concerns where authoritarian practice survived the transition: inside vendor organizations that operate in a competitive electoral system while governing their members through hierarchical, unwritten, and enforceable rules.

Street vending in Mexico City is registered, monitored, taxed, fined, and bargained over by several public authorities at once. The condition is one of institutional density rather than absence, and the disorder follows from the number of authorities holding a claim. A final chapter develops a shadow case of street vending in New York City, where authority over public space is also fragmented but for different institutional reasons, to distinguish what is specific to Mexico City's democratization from what follows more generally when multiple institutions share authority over public space.

The book draws on fieldwork in Mexico City between 2021 and 2024, including interviews with vendors, organization leaders, inspectors, borough staff, and elected officials; observation of negotiation tables; a period selling alongside vendors; and a database of roughly 1,000 street markets matched with location-based data. The next stage adds archival research reconstructing how vending was governed before the transition.
