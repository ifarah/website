Local bureaucracies in Latin America are often depicted as patronage-driven and lacking expertise. This comparative project argues that bureaucracies in large cities operate differently, and that intermediation between the state and organized urban populations is a structural feature of Latin American urban governance rather than a deviation from an ideal Weberian state.

**How Cities Shape Bureaucracies: Rethinking Public Administration from Mexico City and São Paulo** (jointly with Marcela Alonso Ferreira, Sciences Po). We theorize three features of urban agglomeration that set large city bureaucracies apart: administrative scale, which makes them more diverse, specialized, and vertically segmented; the concentration of human capital, which lets political appointments serve as a channel for technical expertise as well as patronage; and dense state and society interactions, which require continuous negotiation with organized civil society and blur the line between administration and politics. Drawing on administrative data from Brazil and Mexico and fieldwork on land regularization and street vending regulation, we show how these features shape who staffs city governments and how policies are made and implemented.

**Bureaucrats, Brokers, and Negotiated Order: Political Intermediation in Latin American Cities** (co-organized with Andrea Urbina, Universidad Técnica Federico Santa María). This proposed session for the 2027 Urban Affairs Association conference in New York convenes research from across Latin American cities on street-level discretion, brokerage and associational politics, bureaucratic careers, multilevel governance, and negotiated enforcement. It is conceived as the first step toward a special issue that builds a comparative vocabulary for what travels across cases and what does not.
