Street vendors across New York City operate under a single legal framework, yet enforcement falls very unevenly across the city. This project asks how street vending enforcement has varied spatially and historically, and what explains that uneven geography. Once underlying commercial intensity is held constant, does enforcement follow organized commercial interests such as Business Improvement Districts, political representation on the City Council, or the fragmented jurisdiction of the agencies that actually issue tickets?

We build a database of geocoded street vending violations adjudicated by the Office of Administrative Trials and Hearings from 1998 to 2025, linked to vendors' home locations, Business Improvement District boundaries, commercial land use, 311 complaints, neighborhood demographics, and a measure of each council member's legislative support for vendors. The long historical record lets us trace how enforcement has shifted across mayoral administrations and fine schedules, while a focused analysis of the period since April 2023, when the Department of Sanitation took over as the lead enforcement agency from the NYPD, isolates the governance mechanisms behind the current geography. The project also follows the city's recent reforms, including the decriminalization of unlicensed vending under Local Law 122 of 2025 and the creation of the Office of Street Vendor Services in 2026.

The larger question is who governs informality in practice: enforcement agencies, organized business interests, or elected representatives. The project also examines who bears the burden of enforcement, by comparing where vendors live with where they are cited. (Jointly with Amélie Dall'Erba.)
